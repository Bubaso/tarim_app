TARIM PORTALI · PRODUCTION DOMAINS

Livestock

https://tarimportali.net/yapay-zeka-dijital-tarim/hayvancilik

In livestock, one large measured effect comes not from sensors but from the genome: in US Holsteins, genomic selection cut the generation interval of sires of bulls from about 7 years to under 2.5 years and raised the annual rate of genetic gain in yield traits by 50-100%. Sensors that follow the herd animal by animal have a weaker record: most welfare technologies for dairy cattle on the market have not been independently validated. AI-based detection of mastitis and lameness looks promising, but the certainty of the pooled evidence is low. In Türkiye, livestock support now encourages genomic testing, and a domestic electronic ear tag system is on the way.

What it does

Data enter livestock farming along two paths. The first is precision livestock farming, which means knowing the herd animal by animal: sensors on the neck or leg measure movement and rumination, milking robots measure the milk at every milking, cameras measure posture; the aim is to catch disease or heat before the human eye does. AI enters this path with models that classify disease or behaviour from sensor data.

The second is breeding: in genomic selection an animal's genetic value is predicted from its DNA, without waiting for its offspring's performance.

What the field says

García-Ruiz and colleagues (2016) measured the effect of genomic selection on the Holstein breed in its seventh year in the United States:

MEASUREBEFORE GENOMIC SELECTIONAFTER
Generation interval, sires of bullsabout 7 yearsunder 2.5 years
Generation interval, dams of bullsabout 4 yearsabout 2.5 years
Annual genetic gain, yield traits—50-100% increase
Annual genetic gain, lowly heritable traits—threefold to fourfold

The most striking effect was on lowly heritable traits such as fertility, lifespan and health: traits that had eroded over the years began to improve rapidly in the breed.

On the sensor side, Stygar and colleagues (2021) reviewed commercial technologies usable for welfare assessment in dairy cattle. Of 1,111 publications, only 42 studies had put 30 tools through independent (external) validation. Of the 129 technologies on the market, only 18 (14%) had been independently validated. The validation rate was 30% for accelerometers, 10% for cameras, 8% for load cells and milk sensors, and 7% for boluses. Lying, standing and rumination were the behaviours validated most often and with the highest performance; health measures such as body condition and mastitis detection fell in the lower-performance group.

Where AI stands

Erhan and Yağanoğlu (2026), writing from Türkiye, pooled in a meta-analysis how accurately AI detects disease and welfare conditions in farm animals against a reference standard. Of 154 full texts, 74 met the criteria, but only 10 studies (7 on mastitis, 3 on lameness) could enter the pooled analysis; most could not be pooled because they reported performance per record or udder quarter rather than per animal. Pooled sensitivity (how many sick animals are caught) was 83.5%, specificity (how many healthy animals are correctly called healthy) 90.1%. The authors' reading is measured: the method is promising, but the size of the differences between studies, the small number of poolable studies and the low certainty of the evidence call for cautious interpretation.

Single studies can report much higher figures. A study by researchers at Muğla Sıtkı Koçman University and the University of Science and Technology in Bydgoszcz, Poland, reports predicting mastitis risk from the milking-robot sensor data of 6,600 cows with 98.1% accuracy (Abdul Ghafoor and Sitkowska, 2021). The meta-analysis shows why the gap between a single study's high figure and the pooled evidence matters. Both results, moreover, measure how accurately disease is detected; how much early detection with sensors or AI changes animal health or productivity on a farm in Türkiye is a question no published assessment has yet answered.

Türkiye

According to the ministry's data for June 2026, Türkiye has 17.6 million cattle, 49.5 million sheep and 11.3 million goats. According to the 2027 Programme, milk production fell from 22.5 million tonnes in 2024 to about 21.4 million tonnes in 2025. The programme aims to develop genetic capacity by spreading artificial insemination; the share of improved breeds in the cattle population was 48.8% in 2025, with 49.5% targeted for 2027.

On the policy side there are two steps. The decision governing livestock support was amended in September 2026, introducing an additional coefficient for breeding heifers that have undergone genomic testing. In 2027, a domestically developed Secure Electronic Ear Tag and Tracking System is to be put into use. On the AI side, the VetAlarm project to develop an AI-based early diagnosis and warning system for animal diseases was approved in 2025.
