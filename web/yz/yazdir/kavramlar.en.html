TARIM PORTALI · FRAMEWORK

Terms: AI, digital farming, precision farming

https://tarimportali.net/yapay-zeka-dijital-tarim/kavramlar

AI, digital farming and precision farming are often used as if they were interchangeable, but the three do not describe the same thing. AI is a family of technologies. Precision farming, by contrast, is a management strategy that measures the variability of fields, plants and animals with data and turns it into decisions; its definition does not require AI, and its core technologies were commercialised before AI became widespread. Digital farming is the umbrella that covers both. The terms can also be confused with one another when translated into Turkish; in Türkiye, ministry documents use "akıllı tarım" (smart farming) as the umbrella term.

Artificial intelligence

The EU's AI Act (2024) defines not "AI" but an "AI system": a machine-based system designed to operate with varying levels of autonomy, which may adapt after deployment and which, for explicit or implicit objectives, infers from the input it receives how to generate outputs such as predictions, content, recommendations or decisions that can influence physical or virtual environments. The definition the OECD updated in November 2023 is almost the same sentence; the OECD's recommendation on AI was first adopted in 2019.

The common criterion of these definitions is inference. According to the Act's recitals, the techniques that enable inference include both machine learning approaches that learn from data and logic- and knowledge-based approaches that infer from encoded knowledge; simple software that executes operations automatically on rules defined solely by people falls outside the definition. According to the OECD's explanatory memorandum, machine learning is a set of techniques that allows machines to improve their performance through exposure to training data; AI covers both machine learning and knowledge-based approaches. In other words, machine learning is part of AI, not AI itself.

The European Commission's guidelines of July 2025 give examples of what does not count as AI:

  • Systems that accelerate well-established optimisation methods such as linear or logistic regression
  • Basic data processing that follows predefined instructions
  • Classical heuristics
  • Simple predictions such as forecasting tomorrow's temperature from last week's average

The guidelines are not binding; only the Court of Justice of the EU can give an authoritative interpretation of the Act.

In Türkiye, the National AI Strategy (2021-2025) defines AI as "bir bilgisayarın veya bilgisayar kontrolündeki bir robotun çeşitli faaliyetleri zeki canlılara benzer şekilde yerine getirme kabiliyeti" (the capability of a computer or a computer-controlled robot to perform various activities in a way similar to intelligent beings) and counts machine learning among AI's sub-fields of application. The document says it uses its terms on the basis of the OECD and EU definitions; but its definition of AI describes a capability, not a system; it defines "YZ Sistemi" (AI system) separately, as a system designed to perform a function by collecting and interpreting data. The Türkiye Artificial Intelligence Action Plan (2026-2030) writes that its glossary was prepared to explain policy concepts, and that concepts requiring legal force will be defined separately in the relevant legislation.

Precision farming

According to the definition the International Society of Precision Agriculture (ISPA) updated in January 2024, precision farming is not a technology but a management strategy: it gathers, processes and analyses temporal, spatial and individual plant and animal data, combines them with other information and supports management decisions according to estimated variability, in order to improve resource use efficiency, productivity, quality, profitability and sustainability. The society's first official definition was written through a process that began in 2018 with feedback from its members and a panel of 46 experts; following a decision in 2023 to expand it, the definition was updated in January 2024. FAO's 2022 definition also frames precision farming as a management strategy.

The US Department of Agriculture's Economic Research Service (ERS) dates the commercialisation of the yield monitor to 1993 and of variable-rate equipment to 1995; in 2023 the same service wrote that AI and machine learning have not yet been deployed at scale in agriculture, so there is little evidence of their contribution to productivity. FAO counts auto-steer guided by satellite positioning as a good example of precision farming technology.

Nor is precision farming the same as variable-rate application. According to Lowenberg-DeBoer and Erickson, precision farming is not one technology but a toolkit from which farmers choose what they need; variable-rate application is only one tool in the kit and, as of 2019, was rarely used on more than 20% of farms. The authors note that there is no clear definition of precision farming, and that one research group compiled 27 different definitions from the scientific literature and the internet. According to a 2016 study for the European Parliament, precision farming can also be practised without machines: especially in developing countries, data are used while tasks such as planting, harvesting and irrigation are done by people.

Digital farming, smart farming, Agriculture 4.0

ERS defines digital agriculture as a transformation that includes the digitalisation and automation of farming tasks, with precision agriculture as its chief element. According to the service, the components of digital agriculture fall into three groups:

  • Data and data collection systems
  • Decision support tools
  • Data-driven equipment and input adjustments

Other bodies order the terms differently. According to a 2020 FAO publication, Agriculture 4.0 is a set of innovations covering precision farming, the Internet of Things and big data; in a 2019 FAO briefing, precision agriculture is one application of the Internet of Things in agriculture. CEMA, the association of Europe's farm machinery industry, has its own classification:

  • Precision farming is Agriculture 3.0, which began when GPS signals were opened to the public.
  • Digital farming is the stage that adds intelligent networks and data management to precision farming technology.
  • Robotics and AI come only with Agriculture 5.0.

The technical annex of the 2016 study for the European Parliament uses smart farming and precision agriculture as synonyms. According to Wolfert and colleagues (2017), smart farming is the use of information and communication technology in the farm management cycle; robots and AI are an expected outcome of this development. A 2016 guide by FAO and the International Telecommunication Union called the same field e-agriculture.

A similarly named term adds to the confusion: climate-smart agriculture. Defined by FAO in 2010, this approach has three objectives: sustainably increasing productivity and incomes, adapting to climate change, and reducing greenhouse gas emissions where possible. According to FAO, climate-smart agriculture is not a single technology or practice that can be applied everywhere.

Usage in Türkiye

In the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry's 2024-2028 Strategic Plan (2025 update), "akıllı tarım uygulamaları" (smart farming applications) is an umbrella term: applications to be spread through the use of technological developments such as product verification, harvest forecasting, AI, geographic information systems and algorithms. The plan includes an activity named "Dijital Dönüşüm ve Tarım 4.0 Hizmetleri" (Digital Transformation and Agriculture 4.0 Services), and a performance indicator tracks the number of farms using climate-friendly smart farming technology systems, with a 2028 target of 585 farms. The National Rural Development Strategy (2024-2028) uses two separate concepts side by side in the phrase "akıllı tarım/iklim akıllı" (smart farming/climate-smart).

The working paper of the digital transformation working group of the Fourth Agriculture and Forestry Council openly acknowledges the confusion: terms such as smart, digital and precision farming can be confused when translated into Turkish from foreign terms; at first experts used "Hassas Tarım" (precision farming), and later "Akıllı Tarım" (smart farming) and "Dijital Tarım" (digital farming) became more widely accepted. The paper calls the precision farming era, which began when GPS was opened to public use, Agriculture 3.0, and writes that the period that began in the 2010s is called "Tarım 4.0", "Akıllı Tarım", "Dijital Tarım" or "E-Tarım" (Agriculture 4.0, smart, digital or e-agriculture). According to a 2025 report by FAO and the EBRD, Agriculture 4.0 is a widely used term in Türkiye; based on a 2020 ministry survey, the report states that fewer than 3% of farmers use smart farming technologies such as remote sensing, sensors, farm or herd management software, precision farming or robotics.
