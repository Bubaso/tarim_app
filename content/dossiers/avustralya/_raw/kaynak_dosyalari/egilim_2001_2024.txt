1. About this report 
The purpose of this report is t o provide insights into trends in how water share holders, irrigators and others have 
been using northern Victorian water market s to access water, manage their water risks and trade water between 
people and places. It provides an update to the last water market trends report (DELWP 2019) and the original market 
trends report (DEPI 2016) by focussing on new data and observed trends since 2018-19, in the context of the historic 
data from previous reports. 
Making this information publicly available is part of the Victorian Government’s commitment to provide transparency 
about changes in how water entitlements are held and used in northern Victoria to help water users make informed 
decisions about their participation in the market. 
Unless otherwise stated, the data presented in this report comes from the Victorian Water Register. Data for periods 
prior to 2007 (i.e. 1991 and 2001) comes from The Value of Water: A Guide to Water Trading in Victoria (DNRE 
2001). Appendix 2 to this report presents tables and charts of data to allow anyone to quote, use and analyse this 
information further. 
2. Context 
Water markets in northern Victoria are a subset of broader markets spanning the southern-connected Murray Darling 
Basin (including the South Australian Murray, NSW Murray and Murrumbidgee systems) . Subject to trading rules, 
water can move between these interconnected systems (or trading zones). 
Water availability and seasonal conditions 
The last water market trends report (DELWP 2019) was released during a period of low rainfall and inflows across 
the southern Murray Darling Basin. Water availability was particularly low in NSW, where general security allocations 
were 0% and 7% in the NSW Murray and Murrumbidgee respectively. 
Dry conditions continued through 2019-20, however water availability began to improve in 2020-21, which was then 
followed by three relatively wet years with high water availability for entitlement holders and widespread flooding in 
northern Victoria. The relatively wet conditions challenged river operators and irrigators with full and spilling storages 
and waterlogged land. The Resource Manager announced 100% seasonal determinations to low-reliability water 
shares in the Victorian Murray for the first time ever in 2021-22, which was then repeated in 2022-23 and 2023-24. 
Regional commodity trends and water use behaviour 
The area of perennial horticulture plantings along the Murray downstream of the Barmah Narrows in NSW, South 
Australia and Victoria has continued to grow, increasing by 23,000 hectares between 2015 and 2021. This increase 
has been most notable for table grapes and nuts, with some crop plantings such as wine grapes declining over this 
period (SunRISE 2022). Expansion of the irrigated area of table grapes and nut s in the region continued between 
2021 and 2023 (SunRISE 2023). This expansion reflects favourable commodity prices and proven farming systems 
for profitable growing of these crops in the region . It is in this context that these horticulturalists can often ‘out 
compete’ other water market participants when water is scarce by offering higher prices for water . The trends data 
in sections 3 and 5Figure 1 of this report highlights that private water users in the Victorian Murray downstream of 
Barmah Narrows (Lower Murray Water ( LMW) diverters and LMW districts), have retained strong and consistent 
water use with the support of large volumes of trade . Since 2010, there has also been a shift in seasonal cropping 
patterns in the Murrumbidgee system driven by commodity prices and the development of colder climate varieties of 
cotton where large areas of irrigated cotton have replaced more traditional rice crops (BoM 2023). While areas of 
irrigated cotton fell in 2019 -20 with low water availability and corresponding high water prices a longside softening 
cotton prices, areas of both irrigated cotton and rice have since increased with improved water availability (BoM 
2023).

Water recovery for the environment 
Water recovery targets from the largest irrigation moderni sation project in Australian history, the Goulburn -Murray 
Water (GMW) Connections Project , were finalised in 2020. Consistent with commitments under the project, this 
resulted in reductions in GMW ’s loss allowance entitlement as a result of water savings from improved efficiency , 
and the issue of new water entitlements to environmental water holders , Melbourne’s urban water retailers, and 
Goulburn Murray Irrigation District (GMID) delivery share holders. 
The purchase of water shares for the environment in northern Victoria under the Commonwealth Government’s 
Restoring the Balance in the Murray-Darling Basin Program, which peaked in 2009-10 to 2011-12, was wrapped up 
by 2018. Between 2018 -19 and 2023-24 there were no water share purchases for the environment in northern 
Victoria. However , in 2023 Commonwealth Government announced plans to purchase additional water towards 
Basin Plan water recovery, and under the Aboriginal Water Entitlements Program, across the Murray-Darling Basin, 
including in northern Victoria . These programs commenced in 2024-25 and are expected to continue into 2025-26 
and 2026-27. Volumes of water shares purchased by the Australian Government after 1 July 2024 can be viewed 
here. 
Water market and Victorian trade rule reform 
Over the period 2018 to 2022, DEECA completed a review of the Goulburn to Murray trade arrangements. The review 
was established because unprecedented volumes of traded water delivered from the Goulburn to the Murray in 2017-
18 and 2018-19 caused environmental damage to the lower Goulburn River with sustained high flows in summer and 
autumn. The review process resulted in the introduction of restrictions on use from tagged accounts when allocation 
trade limits apply (introduced initially in December 2019) and new Goulburn to Murray trade and operating rules 
(introduced initially on 1 July 2021 ). Details of the changes made to the rule can be found Goulburn to Murray 
trade review Long-term rules – fact sheet 1. 
In response to community concerns about unethical behaviour in water market s, the Australian Competition and 
Consumer Commission (ACCC) was directed in 2019 to conduct an inquiry into Murray-Darling Basin water markets, 
including their operation, transparency, regulation, competitiveness and efficiency. This Inquiry was completed in 
2021. The ACCC did not find evidence that investors exercised market power or manipulated markets to increase 
water prices in the Southern Connected Basin. However, the ACCC did find that the perception of misconduct by 
water investors has reduced water users’ confidence in water markets and that information was not readily available 
to robustly assess behaviour and market performance. The ACCC made several recommendations related to water 
market transparency and integrity which Victoria and the other Basin governments have used to develop a roadmap 
for water market reform. 
Two key Victorian market transparency improvements relevant to this report have been made in recent years. Firstly, 
in August 2020, Victoria introduced a new requirement that the ‘reason for trade’ be recorded on all online allocation 
trade applications (NSW and South Australia have both also introduced similar requirements) . This change has 
enabled improved reporting and market information about why people are using allocation trade . The ‘reasons for 
trade’ categories are : standard commercial trade, related party trade, carryover parking, carryover parking return, 
private lease arrangement, allocation trade as part of a water share transfer, forward contract and ‘other’. Secondly, 
in response to community consultation, changes to Victoria’s Water Act 1989 were introduced in July 2024 that 
enable new information to be published about frequent allocation traders (people who make more than 20 commercial 
Murray and/or Goulburn allocation trades in a year). 
Water delivery in the Murray River 
Another issue, indirectly related to water markets, is increasing delivery or shortfall risks in the Murray. There have 
been significant changes across the connected Murray -Darling Basin over the last 10 -20 years that have made it 
increasingly difficult to deliver water in the Murray downstream of the Barmah Narrows. These changes include the 
reduced channel capacity through the Barmah Narrows, less frequent access to water in the Menindee Lakes and 
changing timing and location of water demand.

In addition to working with the other Murray -Darling Basin governments to understand, manage and prepare for 
shortfalls, Victoria introduced in November 2023 a new ‘place of take approvals’ framework to clarify and protect the 
rights of water users to have water delivered down rivers in a shortfall and enable more flexibility for river diverters 
to manage their own delivery risks through the trade of extraction share. The newly introduced extraction share 
market is not covered in this report, due to the lim ited data available to date, however future water market reporting 
will expand to incorporate extraction share trade information.

Since 2018-19, private annual water use has fluctuated between 1,112 GL and 1,751 GL. Water use was lowest in 
2019-20 when dry conditions resulted in low water availability across the southern connected Murray-Darling Basin, 
although notably, use was higher than in the last drought year in 2008 -09. Low-reliability allocations were available 
in the Murray in 2021-22, 2022-23 and 2023 -24, but there is no observable consequent increase in water use in 
those years in this data. There is also no obvious signal of decreased private water use from the acquisition of water 
shares for the environment between 2009 -10 to 2018 ; it is likely that year -to-year climate and water availability 
variations cause sufficient ‘noise’ in the data to mask such a trend if it exists. 
GMW district customers have continued to account for the largest share of private water use in northern Victoria, 
however their use volumes are also the most variable . The decline in water use in 2019 -20 was most evident in 
GMW’s districts where only 531 GL was used (almost half their average annual use). 
LMW diverters and district users had a comparatively small decline in water use in response to the dry conditions. 
The difference in GMW and LMW regional experiences highlights the stark difference between highly variable water 
use in GMW’s region (where irrigation is dominated by mixed farming and dairy) and the more consistent water use 
in LMW’s region (where irrigation is dominated by perennial horticulture).1 
Figure 1 shows that with the exception of 2022 -23 when widespread flooding suppressed demand, use by LMW 
diverters has been relatively stable since 2017 -18, indicating that growth in perennial horticulture plantings during 
this time has not increased overall water use. This contrasts with the trend observed in the 2018 Market Trends 
Report that water use by LMW diverters had increased since 2004-05. It is likely that the stable trend since 2017-
2018 – even during the ‘drought’ years of 18-19 and 19-20 – is influenced by several factors, including the relatively 
mild seasonal conditions since 2020-21, exits of existing farm businesses, and reductions in local seasonal cropping.

4. Water share trade 
Water share ownership 
Trends in water share ownership highlight who has access to water allocations that could be made available on the 
market, and how that has changed over time. Owners can choose to link their water shares to land which may indicate 
their intent to use the water allocations on that land, although does not prevent them selling allocations on the market. 
Owners may choose not to link their water shares to land for a wide variety of reasons, including financial and 
accounting purposes2, irrigators wanting flexibility to move water for use on their land and investors selling water 
allocations to generate an annual return.

Since 2018, there has been no significant change in the proportion of high-reliability water share volumes in northern 
Victoria held by the environment (30%), water corporations (2%) and private owners (68%). However, the total 
volume of high-reliability water shares in northern Victoria has increased by 129 GL during this period as a result of 
entitlement changes reflecting water recovered under the GMW Connections Project. 3 
Most privately held high-reliability water shares remain linked to land, however this volume has continued to decline, 
reducing from 1,390 GL at 30 June 2018 to 1,262 GL at 30 June 2024. The rate of decline has been higher in irrigation 
districts than for diverters across GMW and LMW regions 4. At 30 June 2024, 18% of high -reliability water shares in 
northern Victoria were privately owned and not linked to land compared to 11% at 30 June 2018. Use of allocations 
made to water shares not linked to land is explored further in Section 6 of this report.

Water share prices have historically been relatively similar across different water sources. However, since 2018

Low reliability water share prices 
Figure 4 shows how the median prices of low-reliability water shares has varied since unbundling in July 2007. 
Figure 4: Low-reliability water share median prices (6-month rolling average) 
 
In this period, low -reliability water share median prices have followed a similar pattern to high-reliability prices – 
broadly stable until approximately 2018-19, followed by a series of significant increases. This price increase has been 
most significant in Murray zone 7, where low-reliability water share prices increased from $444 per ML in July 2018 
to $1,861 per ML in June 2024. Low reliability price rises may be influenced by a growing recognition of the value of 
these water shares to carry over water in average or dry years, as well as receiving allocations in wetter years like 
2021-22, 2022-23 and 2023-24. 
Water share prices in recent years have likely also been supported by emergence of the “carryover parking” market 
where water share holders have been able to generate new revenue by leasing water shares (representing additional 
carryover capacity) to others seeking to increase the ability to hold on to their unused allocations. This new market 
is explored in further detail later in section 5.

5. Water allocation trade 
Allocation trade volumes 
Annual allocation trade volumes6 have generally increased year on year since unbundling occurred in 2007 -08; the 
exceptions to this occurred in some of the drier years with low early season allocations (e.g. 2013-14 and 2018-19) 
(Figure 5). Over this period, trade volumes as a proportion of total allocation available (including volumes carried 
over and new season allocations) has been trending upwards, indicating ongoing growth of water markets as water 
users increasingly use allocation markets to maximise the value of thei r entitlements and meet their water needs 
within the water year.

Figure 7 shows that allocation prices have continued to be highest during drought and low water availability periods, 
and lower during wet years with high water availability and low water use. 
Record high allocation prices were observed in 2019 -20 when water availability fell to the lowest levels since the 
Millennium Drought. Several factors contributed to these higher prices, including two years of hot, dry conditions, 
very low water availability in NSW (much lower than in 2008-09), the reduced volume of high-reliability water shares 
held for consumptive purposes (due to purchases for the environment), and growth in perennial horticultural 
industries with a high willingness to pay for allocation. 
The relatively low allocation prices observed since 2020 -21 reflect high water availability (high carryover volumes 
and 100% allocations, including for low -reliability water shares). In 2022 -23, allocation prices were particularly low 
(the median annual price in the Goulburn and Victorian Murray was $20/ML) due to suppressed demand following 
heavy rainfall and flooding in spring and early summer that offset irrigation needs and damaged crops in some 
regions. The last time allocation prices have been as low as 2022-23 was in 2011-12, when water availability (new 
season allocation plus carryover) was even higher.

Interzone allocation trade restrictions and price differentials 
Trade limits are necessary to protect entitlement holders and waterway health from potential negative impacts. 
When these limits are reached and allocation trade between zones is restricted, allocation prices in the different 
zones can diverge in response to different supply and demand conditions. 
Two of the most influential restrictions on allocation trade in northern Victoria in recent years have been the limits 
on trade from upstream to downstream of Barmah and from the Goulburn to the Murray10. Data showing when 
these trade restrictions have been binding is presented in Figure 10 alongside median weekly allocation prices for 
the largest trading zones on each side of the restriction (Murray zone 6 and Goulburn zone 1A as the largest zones 
where trade is restricted from, and Murray zone 7 as the largest Victorian zone where is restricted to) . 
 
Figure 10 highlights a price divergence between the restricted zones whenever these limits were in effect, with high 
prices in the zone where trade is restricted into and lower prices in the zone where trade is restricted f rom. The 
divergence was most significant in 2019 -20 when there was low water availability and high prices across northern 
Victoria and both limits remained closed for most of the year. In mid-November 2019 the median weekly price peaked 
at $960 per ML in the Murray zone 7 compared to $650 per ML in the Murray zone 6 and $660 per ML in the Goulburn 
zone 1A. These price differences reinforce the strong demand for water in the Murray downstream of Barmah and 
demonstrate the high willingness to pay from these water users.

Carryover parking 
Victorian carryover provisions have opened up opportunities for commercial arrangements where water share holders 
carry over water against their water share on behalf of someone else for a fee. This provides water share holders , 
water users and market participants more options and more flexibility to manage their water, and water share holders 
the opportunity to generate more income from their asset . This practice is commonly referred to as “carryover 
parking”. Since August 2020, all online allocation trades have been reported with a ‘reason for trade’ including 
carryover parking (commercial trade to temporarily move water to another account to be held and carried over for 
the following year) and carryover parking return (the return trade in the new water year back into the original seller 
account). 
Figure 12 draws on the four years of ‘reason for trade’ data to present how much commercial allocation trade has 
been for carryover parking compared to other reasons, and how the prices for carryover parking have differed from 
other commercial trade prices.

carryover parking between 2020 -21 and 2022-23 suggest there has been increasing uptake as more people have 
become aware of this emerging market. The slight reduction in carryover parking volumes in 2023 -24, when there 
was less ‘unused’ water available at the end of the year than in 2022-23, suggest that water availability at the end of 
the year may be a key driver influencing the volumes actually traded for the purpose of carryover parking. 
The prices paid for carryover parking have gradually increased over the four years of data (from a median price of 
$45/ML in 2020-21 to a median price of $70/ML in 2023-24) indicating increased recognition of the value of carryover 
parking to water share holders and water users. The prices paid for carryover parking differ considerably from other 
commercial trades. In the two wetter years (2022 -23 and 2023 -24), when high water availability meant there was 
limited demand for within year water use, the median price for other commercial allocation trades was very low 
($20/ML and $40/ML respectively), and carryover prices were higher ($65/ML and $70/ML).

Water shares not linked to land 
This section describes the trends in use of available water by holders of private water shares that are not linked to 
land. Allocation made to these water shares is either carried over or written off if it is not traded. Consistent with data 
presented in previous reports, holders of private water shares that are not linked to land tend to sell most of their 
available water commercially, making it available for irrigators to purchase and use (Figure 13). These holders (on 
average) also consistently move some allocation to another account for $0 in most years, which may reflect transfers 
into accounts for use on the account holders’ own farm, or to a related party . 
The proportion of available water that is carried over into the next year varies somewhat between years, but in recent 
years fairly closely mirrors the same statistic for private water users; that is, holders of water shares not linked to land 
tend to carry over about the same proportion of their available water that private water users do (Figure 13). 
This data indicates that holders of water shares not linked to land are continuing to make most of the allocation they 
hold available for water users on the market.

Frequent Traders 
To increase market transparency, Victoria has commenced reporting on ‘frequent traders’ in the Goulburn and Murray 
systems. A frequent trader is defined as the owner of a Victorian allocation account that has undertaken more than 
20 commercial allocation trades, within, to, or from the Goulburn and Murray systems during the year excluding 
interstate trades 14. Quarterly updates identifying these holders are now published on the Victorian Water Register 
website, linked here. 
Qualifying to be on the frequent trader list does not indicate any wrongdoing. Many parties rely on water allocation 
markets as part of their business model. The list simply provides transparency about account holders who trade 
relatively frequently in the Goulburn and Murray systems, as these traders have the potential to have more influence 
in the market. 
Analysis shows that since 2015-16 there have been between 48 and 104 frequent trader accounts each year 
(Figure 15). This number was higher in dry years when prices were higher (2018-19 and 2019-20) and lower in the 
wet, low-price years (such as 2022-23). These fluctuations are broadly in line with fluctuations in the total number 
of commercial trades, which was also higher in those drier years. It is worth noting that when allocation prices 
were highest in 2019-20, frequent traders sold a smaller proportion (33%) of the total commercial trade than in 
years with lower prices (including 2022-23 (40%) and 2023-24 (42%). 
Between 25% and 42% of the total volume of commercial trade in the Victorian Murray and Goulburn has been 
sold from frequent trader accounts each year since 2015-16. This is notable given that frequent trader accounts 
make up approximately 1% of the total number of accounts trading each year ( Table 1). When counting any trades 
where a frequent trader is the buyer or the seller (i.e. trades involving at least one frequent trader account), this 
rises to 48 to 65% of the total volume (Figure 15). There is no clear trend across the time period. The high 
proportion can be explained by two factors: 1. by definition, these are accounts that trade more frequently than all 
other accounts; 2. the average parcel size of trades by frequent trader accounts is approximately twice as large 
(164 ML) as the average parcel size of all other trades (85 ML).

7. Conclusions 
This report provides an update since the last Water Market Trends report (DEECA 2019) , with a primary focus on 
new data and trends since 2017-18, in the context of a longer history of Victoria’s northern water markets. The report 
provides new data and discussion, both on topics from the previous report but also emerging issues and topics of 
interest to market participants. 
Private rural water use in GMW districts has continued to account for the largest segment of private use in northern 
Victorian declared river systems , but also the most variable year to year. The trend identified in the last version of 
this report of increasing total use by the group of LMW diverters has stabilised; looking at the longer period of record 
there is no material trend evident since 2012-13. 
A look at water share trade median prices has shown a significant diversion of Murray downstream of Barmah (Zone 
7) water share prices above other trading zones since 2018, likely reflecting the growth of perennial horticulture 
demand in that region and increasing allocation trade restrictions into the region. 
Water allocation trade overall volumes continued to increase year on year . The LMW diverter group is the private 
water user group most dependent on trade to meet their water needs. 
Updated information is also provided on the behaviour of holders of water shares not linked to land; i.e. what they do 
with their allocation each year. 
Finally, two key improvements in market transparency , the collection of ‘reason for trade’ data and a new analysis 
on market participants who meet the criteria for ‘frequent traders ’, have both provided new insights to this report. 
‘Reason for trade’ helps to characterise trends such as the emergence of a market for carryover parking . The 
availability of this data also improves other analyses such as allocation price trend s. Data showing the behaviour of 
frequent traders in the market highlights that while this group of organisations and individuals is involved in an 
outsized proportion of traded volume, there is no evidence of systematic differences in the price of these trades 
compared to other, less frequent, traders.

Appendix 1 – Water shares linked to land 
A new method for reporting on water shares “linked to land” has been introduced in this report in response to changes 
to the Water Act 1989. The new method better reflects where allocations made to water shares are approved for use 
on land. This Appendix explains the legislative changes, the new reporting method s, and how the y compare to 
previous methods. 
Legislative changes – removal of association 
Changes to the Water Act 1989 were introduced on 20 November 2023 which removed the ability to associate a 
water share with land (under Part 3A, Division 10 ). Association was one of the ways that the water under a water 
share could be approved to be used on land, and was originally put in place to manage the following limits: 
• no more than 10% of the water share volume in a system could be owned without being linked to land (this 
limit was abolished in 2009), and 
• no more than 4% of the water share volume in an irrigation area could be moved out the area in a year (this 
limit was abolished in 2014). 
When unbundling occurred in northern Victoria (1 July 2007) , all water shares were associated with land . Holders 
could choose to disassociate their water shares from land (and associate it with other land if they chose to) subject 
to the above limits (when they were in effect). 
Association was reflected in the Victorian Water Register by a direct connection between the water share and the 
water-use licence or registration on the relevant land (refer red line in figure below). Associating a water share 
provided approval to use water allocated to the water share on the specified land. However, such approval could 
also be obtained via an approval under section 64K of the Water Act 1989. Section 64K approvals are represented 
in the Victorian Water Register by a connection between a wa ter share and an allocation account with a connected 
water-use licence or registration (refer blue lines in figure below). 
 
Over time, water users increasingly opted to rely on section 64K approvals for their approvals to use water. There 
were different fees for water shares associated with land, so over time more and more people chose to link their 
water share to land without formally associating it under Part 3A, Division 10 of the Water Act 1989. As such, reporting 
on water shares associated with land increasingly became a poor measure of where water shares were approved to 
be used. 
The legislation changes made in 2023 simplified the approvals required to use allocation at a particular place. This 
included removing the unnecessary ability to associate water shares with land – reflecting the fact that association 
was no longer required to manage the 10% and 4% limits and was no longer a meaningful indication of where water 
share allocations were planned to be used . The new approach to approvals to use water share allocations are now 
consistent – with all w ater users (allocation account holders with a linked water -use licence/registration) having a 
general place of take approval issued under Part 4AA for every place (service point) they take water.

New reporting method – water shares linked to land 
Previous market trend reporting (in 2016 and 2019) reported on water shares “tied to land” based on whether the 
water shares were “associated” to a water-use licence/registration. This report uses a new method to report on water 
shares “linked to land” based on whether a water share is linked to an allocation account with a general place of take 
approval (i.e. the allocation account has a link to a water-use licence/registration). 
Similar to previous reports, the data reported for each water year is based on the active links recorded in the Victorian 
Water Register on 30 June of each year. 
Figure 17 compares these two reporting methods. It shows the volume of high-reliability water shares ‘linked to land’ 
(new method) and the volume ‘tied to land’ (old method). The old method of reporting was not an accurate picture of 
the volume of water shares linked to land as more water share holders chose to disassociate their water shares from 
land, even though they retained other approvals to use that water on land. The inaccuracy became more significant 
over time. The new method provides a more reliable indication of when water shares are linked to land , and aligns 
with the 2023 legislation changes, and the single approach to approvals to use water on land going forwards.

New reporting method – location of take under water shares 
Previous market trend reporting (in 2016 and 2019) reported on the location of water shares “tied to land” based on 
the delivery system recorded on the water share. This is no longer appropriate because the delivery system on the 
water share was derived from an associated water-use licence/registration, and as discussed above, this data was 
becoming increasingly inaccurate. Additionally, now that the ability to associate a water share has been removed, 
the delivery system data on a water share is no longer being maintained. 
A new approach to reporting on where a water share is approved to be used is adopted for this report, that derives 
the delivery system from the water-use licence/registrations linked to the water share’s allocation account. Based on 
2023-24 data, most high -reliability water shares are linked to a single delivery system. However, where a water 
share’s allocations are approved for take at multiple places we have made some simplifying assumptions to enable 
reporting on the location of take. Where a water shar e is linked to multiple delivery systems, the water share is 
reported as being located in the delivery system where the most water has been used in the last two years.15
