# Sensors and IoT

The sensor's best-measured contribution to farming comes where it replaces a calendar or a uniform dose with a measurement: according to a review of studies from the United States, irrigation timed by soil moisture sensors saves an average of **38%** of water compared with traditional methods. But savings do not turn into profit on every field, most commercial devices in the barn have not been independently validated, and the connection that would carry the readings is weakest on small farms. No published study has measured the effect of agricultural sensor networks on farming in Türkiye.

## Measurement instead of the calendar

How much a sensor gains depends on what it replaces: in the review by Datta and Taghvaeian (2023), the saving against irrigating by canopy temperature is only **1%**. What most determines a sensor's accuracy is calibration. Gains are measured in nitrogen too: according to a review of **26** studies in winter wheat, sensor-based nitrogen management raises nitrogen use efficiency by about **10.4%** (Aula and colleagues, 2020). In dryland maize in Nebraska, sensors cut nitrogen by an average of **38.7** kg per hectare with no yield loss on **77%** of the fields; but a profit advantage appeared on only **35%** of them (Kortbein and colleagues, 2026).

The outcome, too, is often decided not by the sensor itself but by the institutions and people around it. In Mexico's Yaqui Valley, the hand-held GreenSeeker sensor brought wheat farmers **37** dollars of extra profit per hectare, excluding the cost of the device and technician; even so, its use declined in the 2015-2016 season. RTI's report lists among the possible reasons the reluctance of credit unions that also sell fertiliser. In Zimbabwe, maize water productivity rose by more than **100%** among irrigators given sensors, but also among their neighbours without them, who learned by watching those who had them (Moyo and colleagues, 2020).

## The barn devices' record

According to Stygar and colleagues (2021), of the **129** technologies on the market for assessing dairy cattle welfare, only **18** (**14%**) had been independently validated; the rate was **30%** for accelerometers, **10%** for cameras and **7%** for boluses. Lying, standing and rumination were the behaviours validated most often and most successfully; health measures such as mastitis detection fell in the lower-performance group. One study reports predicting mastitis risk in **6,600** cows from milking-robot sensor data with **98.1%** accuracy; in a meta-analysis pooling AI-based detection of mastitis and lameness, however, the certainty of the evidence is low (Erhan and Yağanoğlu, 2026).

## Where the connection ends

A sensor does no work until it can pass its readings to a network. According to the global analysis by Mehrabi and colleagues (2021), only **24-37%** of farms smaller than **1** hectare are covered by 3G or 4G, against **74-80%** of those over **200** hectares. In the review by Miller and colleagues (2025), the obstacles facing sensor networks are likewise less about algorithms than about high infrastructure costs, limited interoperability, rural connectivity constraints and data privacy concerns. In Türkiye the state carries part of rural connectivity: service was brought with public funds to **5,565** settlements that operators are not obliged to serve; what this has brought to agriculture has not been measured and published.

## State weather stations, university prototypes

One official measuring network that runs continuously in the field in Türkiye belongs to meteorology: data from **1,717** automatic stations are turned into agricultural products such as frost warnings. Academic work on agricultural sensors, by contrast, is at the level of adoption surveys and prototypes. A tractor tracking device running on a LoRaWAN network, developed at Çukurova University, had hardware costing **55** dollars at May 2020 prices, while a tractor manufacturer's 4G device cost **795** dollars; but data transmission was tested with dummy data (Civelek, 2022). For a wireless moisture sensor tried with **9** growers in a joint project of Ege University and Wageningen, the project leaflet reports water savings of **13-24%**; the company that developed the sensor is among the leaflet's authors, and the figure is a project statement.
