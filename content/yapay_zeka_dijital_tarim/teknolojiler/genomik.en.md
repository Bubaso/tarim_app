# Genomics and breeding

Genomic selection means predicting a plant's or an animal's genetic value from its DNA markers, without waiting for field trials or offspring, and selecting on that basis. This branch has large measured effects: in US Holsteins, genomic selection cut the generation interval of sires of bulls from about **7** years to under **2.5** years and raised the annual rate of genetic gain in yield traits by **50-100%**. What the measured gains in plants and animals have in common is time: selection can happen earlier and generations get shorter. AI enters this chain through the genomic prediction model, and there it has not yet shown a clear edge over classical models. The Türkiye-based winter wheat breeding programme has turned to markers and speed breeding, and livestock support now encourages genomic testing; but no published assessment has measured how genomic selection or AI affects variety development time or genetic gain in a breeding programme in Türkiye.

## The common denominator: time

The slowest link in breeding is testing: to see a line's yield, it has to be sown in different places for years. In the conventional system of the International Winter Wheat Improvement Program, **9-10** years pass from cross to international distribution. In the winter wheat study by Michel and colleagues (2016), prediction based on field measurements alone was not very accurate for grain yield (correlation **0.21**); genomic selection models were more accurate across years (**0.39**), and selection combining the two raised prediction accuracy by **20%**.

In animals the time gained shows directly in the generation interval: according to García-Ruiz and colleagues (2016), by the seventh year of genomic selection in the United States the generation interval of dams of bulls had also fallen, from about **4** years to about **2.5**. In lowly heritable traits such as fertility, lifespan and health, the annual rate of genetic gain rose threefold to fourfold; traits that had eroded over the years began to improve rapidly.

Speed breeding pursues the same gain by another route: by controlling light and temperature it can reach up to **6** generations a year in spring wheat, durum wheat, barley, chickpea and pea, and **4** in canola, against **2-3** in a normal glasshouse (Watson and colleagues, 2018).

## Deep learning's test: data

According to the review by Montesinos-López and colleagues (2021), deep learning showed no clear superiority in predictive power over conventional genomic models in the plant and animal breeding literature. It captures non-linear patterns better and can integrate data from different sources, but its first requirement is high-quality and sufficiently large training data; the authors stress that it should be applied to large data sets. For a programme with little data, its advantage is therefore uncertain.

An example from Türkiye stays at early-stage screening: a study by five universities with Polish partners measured the drought response of tetraploid wheat genotypes at germination in the laboratory and modelled it with four machine learning techniques (Benlioğlu and colleagues, 2024). The results belong to the laboratory and the germination stage, not to yield in the field.

## From a wheat programme to a heifer coefficient

The International Winter Wheat Improvement Program was set up in **1986** between the Government of Türkiye and CIMMYT; according to an assessment published in 2019, **93** varieties from the programme had been released in **11** countries. Until recently it remained largely a conventional breeding programme: in 2017 molecular markers were analysed on a germplasm set of more than **1,500**, and in 2018 a shorter route was started with a speed breeding facility in İzmir that allows two to three generations a year. Its stated future strategy is to combine conventional breeding with high-throughput phenotyping, genomic tools and speed breeding.

The 2027 Programme counts plant breeding as strategic, but its agricultural measures contain no item dedicated to genomic selection or to AI in breeding. In the programme's agriculture chapter, genomics appears only in livestock: the additional support coefficient introduced in September 2026 for breeding heifers that have been genomically tested.
