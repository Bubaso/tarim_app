# 28 · Stories from the field

In East Africa, a phone app that works without internet identified cassava diseases more accurately than extension officers. In England, a barley field was grown from sowing to harvest without anyone entering it; in Morocco, irrigation planned by satellite produced almost the same yield with less water. In the stories of growers and fields that actually use the technology, though, the outcome is often mixed. Türkiye's story is spraying drones.

## Tanzania and Kenya: diagnosis on the phone

PlantVillage Nuru is a phone app developed as a public good by Penn State University's PlantVillage project, FAO, the International Institute of Tropical Agriculture (IITA), CIMMYT and others: a deep learning model diagnoses diseases and pests from leaf symptoms without needing an internet connection. Mrisho and colleagues (2020) compared the app with extension officers and farmers in Tanzania's Mkuranga District and Kenya's Busia County, on two viral diseases and one pest of cassava.

The 2020 version of Nuru identified symptoms with **65%** accuracy; extension officers reached **40-58%** and farmers **18-31%**. Looking at six leaves per plant raised accuracy to **74-88%**. Its weaknesses were measured too: with single leaves, accuracy for brown streak disease was only **21%**, and for mild symptoms **13-37%**; the model's first version dropped to **30-41%** on three other phones. Two weeks of use raised extension officers' diagnostic skill only slightly. The evaluators included researchers from IITA, one of the organisations that developed the app; the app's effect on yields or incomes was not measured.

## England: a field no one entered

The Hands Free Hectare project of Harper Adams University and the company Precision Decisions set out to grow a field from sowing to harvest using only autonomous vehicles and drones. A lightweight tractor did the spraying, drilling and rolling, and a combine harvester brought in the crop. Drones with multispectral and colour cameras imaged the field from above; a small scout vehicle filmed at crop level and took samples.

The 2017 harvest yielded **4.5** tonnes of spring barley. The government's innovation agency, Innovate UK, put the predicted yield at **5** tonnes and announced the result as a demonstration that a whole crop can be grown from start to finish without anyone entering the field; the whole project cost less than **200,000** pounds. The team had to shift spraying and harvest times because of rain, and for safety did not let the tractor get too close to the combine.

The project later grew into a Hands Free Farm of several fields. According to the project's own posts, in 2022 it harvested an average of **9.5** tonnes per hectare from **5** hectares of wheat and an average of **4.03** tonnes per hectare from spring barley; part of the barley was lost where the crop had gone flat. These are the results of a demonstration farm; no comparison with a conventionally farmed field has been published.

## India: extension by video

In the Indian state of Bihar, Digital Green, a non-governmental organisation known for video training aimed at hard-to-reach women farmers, added locally filmed videos to the government's face-to-face rice training. In a randomised controlled trial by Baul and colleagues (2024) in the Nalanda, Muzaffarpur and Purnia districts, **280** villages were assigned to training with videos and **120** to face-to-face training alone.

The average effects were large and positive, but not statistically significant after correcting for multiple comparisons. According to the authors' Bayesian estimate, the videos raised output by **5-12%**, yields by **12-18%** and estimated profits by **9-24%**; the extra cost of the videos is **96** rupees per farmer. The effect on input use was little to none. The trial has no pure control group: it compares training with and without videos.

## Zimbabwe: a sensor and learning from neighbours

In two smallholder irrigation schemes in Zimbabwe's semi-arid regions, Mkoba and Silalatshani, a project running from 2013 to 2017 gave **20** irrigators in each scheme a soil moisture sensor array and two wetting front detectors (Moyo and colleagues, 2020). Farmers switched to less frequent and shorter irrigation and kept nutrients in the root zone.

Water productivity in maize rose by more than **100%**, but among farmers both with and without sensors. The authors attribute this to social learning: those without sensors made the same changes by watching and talking to those who had them. Their conclusion is just that: improving production on small farms cannot be achieved simply by introducing appropriate technology, however good it is.

## Mexico: a profitable sensor that was dropped

In the Yaqui Valley, Mexico's largest wheat-growing region, CIMMYT and Oklahoma State University developed a cheap handheld sensor (GreenSeeker) that looks at the crop mid-season and gives a field-specific nitrogen recommendation. According to a 2017 RTI report, the sensor had been used on more than **48,000** hectares in total since the pilot phase in 2006 and brought farmers **37** dollars of extra profit per hectare; the cost of the device and the technician is not included in that figure. A 2022 peer-reviewed study by the same team estimates that on nearly **2,000** farmer fields covering more than **60,000** hectares in three regions of Mexico, the sensor brought a total of **2.6** million dollars in extra profit and avoided more than **14,300** tonnes of CO2-equivalent emissions. Farmers who did not follow the recommendations made lower profits.

Even so, adoption fell rather than expanded in the 2015-2016 season. The report lists possible reasons: the farmer credit unions, which also sell fertiliser, were reluctant to approve a change that meant using less of it; funding for the technicians' sensor-based advice was shrinking; the credit unions' umbrella organisation changed its leadership; and the same season brought the worst climatic conditions in more than **35** years. The report's conclusion: profit and environmental benefit were not enough to sustain adoption. The 2022 study also writes that the scheme ran on subsidies and that further efforts would be needed to make it sustainable.

## Morocco: an irrigation schedule from satellites

On the Haouz plain near Marrakech, two plots of durum wheat in a farmer's field were monitored side by side in the 2012/13 season: the farmer irrigated one by habit, while irrigation of the other was planned with a method driven by Sentinel-2-like satellite images (Le Page and colleagues, 2014). The test plot got off to a bad start, as a soil crust prevented good emergence; but it caught up and produced almost the same grain yield with **14%** less irrigation water. The result comes from a single field in a single season; the authors see it as an opportunity for areas under flood irrigation in the semi-arid Mediterranean.

## New Zealand: virtual fencing

On pasture-based dairy farms in New Zealand's Canterbury and Waikato regions, cows wear GPS-enabled collars; the system uses sound or electrical cues to limit the grazing area and move the herd. Eastwood and colleagues (2026) interviewed **20** dairy farmers who had been using this virtual fencing for **1-3** years, in 2023 and 2024 (**10** in each region).

The cows adapted to the system within a few days to two weeks; farm changes such as modifying laneways, adding water troughs and removing permanent fences took longer. Farmers described saving time and gaining more control over grazing and the herd; some said the time saved let them reduce a full or part-time employee. But the farmers themselves found it hard to put a financial return on the investment. High investment costs, being locked into the technology, risks to animal health from GPS error or device failure, and the pressure of always being connected were also raised. All the farmers interviewed used an early-stage system from a single brand, Halter; the study was funded by New Zealand's dairy farmers through DairyNZ.

## Türkiye: spraying drones

One of the digital farming technologies used over large areas in Türkiye is the spraying drone. According to an online survey of **71** operators providing this service, carried out in May-August 2026 (İtmeç, 2026), **67.1%** of the businesses spray **500** hectares or more a year; spraying is mainly on wheat (**88.4%**) and maize (**76.8%**) fields. The gaps in practice are clear: only **14.1%** of operators calibrate before every application; **98.6%** take wind into account, but only **16.9%** take relative humidity into account; spraying is often done in or near beekeeping areas (**78.6%**) and in residential areas (**61.4%**). The most frequently reported problem is wind drift (**80.3%**). The survey rests on what operators reported; it does not check the products used, the target pests or the permits.

In Türkiye, drone spraying is not permitted for every crop, pest or type of application. At an evaluation meeting of the General Directorate of Food and Control in March 2026, applications against the **15** harmful organisms for which drone use is permitted, and the permit procedures of provincial directorates, were discussed. An ethnographic study of farmers using drones in western Türkiye (Kaya, 2024) describes how drones measure and visualise a farm's condition and let farmers apply chemicals accordingly; but because of their high cost and their poor fit with some farms' ecologies and existing technologies, they are not equally available to all farmers, and using them requires extra steps to comply with national security rules. These studies show how drones are used in the field; there is, however, no published independent field study from Türkiye measuring the effect of a digital tool on a farm's yields, water use or income.

## What the stories have in common

The technology often works technically. But the outcome is often decided not by the technology but by the institutions and people around it: in Mexico, adoption of a profitable sensor declined, and the possible reasons the report lists include credit unions that also sell fertiliser and shrinking funds; in Zimbabwe, neighbours learned without sensors; in India the effects are large but statistically uncertain; in New Zealand farmers see the benefit but find it hard to put a financial value on it; in Türkiye drones are used over large areas, but good practice is not always followed in the field.
