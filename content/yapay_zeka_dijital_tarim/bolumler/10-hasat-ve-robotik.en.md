# 10 · Harvesting and robotics

> **Summary.** The hardest job at harvest is picking selectively: seeing the ripe fruit, finding the one behind the leaf, detaching it without bruising. That is why harvesting robots long failed to leave the laboratory: in a review published in 2014, the **50** robots examined had an average harvest success of **66%** and took **33** seconds per fruit; performance had not improved in three decades and none had been commercialised. A pepper-harvesting robot tested in a commercial greenhouse picked **61%** of the fruit when the crop was grown to suit it, and **18%** under the greenhouse's usual conditions. According to a 2022 review, widespread use in orchards is still not in sight. In Türkiye, research is so far at the stage of recognising fruit in images.

## What it does

Harvesting is two different worlds of machinery. In grain, a combine harvests the whole field in one pass. In fruit and vegetables, the crop does not ripen all at once and harvesting is selective: for high-value crops such as apples, tomatoes and broccoli this work is still mainly done by hand, and it is one of the most labour-intensive and expensive tasks in agriculture. Labour shortage is one of the reasons for the interest in robots.

A selective harvesting robot has to do three things at once: recognise the fruit in the camera image and locate it, reach it with its arm through leaves and branches, and detach it without damage. AI comes in at the first task, recognising fruit in images. The difficulty lies in an environment that is foreign to a robot: variation from plant to plant, incomplete information, and safety.

## What the field says

Bac and colleagues (2014) compared **50** harvesting robots developed for high-value crops on the same measures:

| Measure (average of 50 robots) | Value |
|---|---|
| Locating the fruit | **85%** |
| Detachment | **75%** |
| Harvest (the whole task) | **66%** |
| Fruit damage | **5%** |
| Peduncle damage | **45%** |
| Time per fruit | **33** seconds |

The fastest was a kiwi robot: **1** second. But the review's main finding lies elsewhere: the performance of harvesting robots had not improved in the past three decades, and none of the **50** robots had been commercialised. The authors' first recommendation was therefore to simplify the task.

The pepper-harvesting robot SWEEPER showed what that recommendation means in practice (Arad and colleagues, 2020). The robot was tested on **262** fruits over four weeks in a commercial greenhouse. Picking one fruit took **24** seconds on average, about half of it logistics; in the laboratory, running the arm faster brought this down to **15** seconds. Harvest success was **61%** when the crop was grown in the conditions best suited to the robot, and **18%** under the greenhouse's usual conditions. The authors' conclusion: success in robotic harvesting depends on finding crop conditions and varieties that suit the robot.

Zhou and colleagues' 2022 review stands in the same place: despite significant progress in the field, widespread use of harvesting robots in orchards is still not in sight; the authors analyse why existing robots perform inadequately.

## Where AI stands

AI's contribution is the robot's eye: finding the fruit in a camera image and judging its ripeness. But the ranking in the review suggests the bottleneck is the hand, not the eye: locating (**85%**) succeeds more often than detaching (**75%**), and detaching more often than the whole harvest (**66%**). The pepper robot's drop to **18%** in the usual greenhouse says the same: unless leaves, branches and fruit are arranged to suit the robot, a better eye is not enough on its own.

## Türkiye

On the machinery side, harvesting in Türkiye rests on conventional mechanisation. According to the ministry's data there were **1,610,133** tractors and **21,116** combine harvesters in 2025; **49.3%** of the combines were 10 years old or younger, and **71%** of the tractors had 35-70 horsepower.

The 2027 Programme writes that the small-scale, fragmented and scattered land structure of holdings makes effective use of mechanisation and modern production techniques harder (§6). A rural development decision of December 2025 and a communiqué of April 2026 also aim to spread robotics and automation technologies; grants cover **50-70%** of the project amount, depending on the type of investment.

On the research side, harvesting-robot work in Türkiye is at the image-recognition stage. In 2024, Kahya and colleagues at Tekirdağ Namık Kemal University trained deep-learning object-recognition models for harvesting eggplant and courgette. What was measured is the models' success in recognising fruit in validation images — not whether a robot detached fruit in the field.

What this dossier's scan did not find is **a published trial in which a harvesting robot picked fruit in a field or greenhouse in Türkiye and its success was measured.**
