# 20 · Sensors and agricultural IoT

> **Summary.** Sensors in the field do no work until they are connected to a network, and the network lags in rural areas: according to the ITU, **82%** of urban dwellers used the internet in 2026, against **63%** in rural areas; 5G reaches **70%** of the urban population and **45%** of the rural population. Small farms lag even further: according to a global analysis, only **24-37%** of farms smaller than **1** hectare are covered by 3G or 4G, against **74-80%** of those over **200** hectares. In Türkiye the state carries part of rural connectivity: service was brought with public funds to **5,565** settlements that operators are not obliged to serve commercially. Machine-to-machine (M2M) subscriptions number **15.2** million and 5G started on 1 April 2026; but agriculture's share of these figures is unknown. The ministry's indicator for farms using smart farming technology stayed at **0** against a target of **410** in 2025, because the grant scheme had not started. There is no published assessment measuring the effect of agricultural sensor and IoT networks in Türkiye.

## What it does

The agricultural internet of things connects measuring devices in fields, greenhouses and barns (soil moisture, temperature, leaf wetness, animal movement, machine position) to a central point over a network. The data are collected on a platform, and AI derives irrigation, spraying or disease warnings from them. Every link in the chain is a cost: device, battery, connection, maintenance. This page looks at that infrastructure, especially rural connectivity; sensor-based irrigation is covered in §7 and sensors in livestock farming in §12.

## What the field says

According to the 2026 estimate of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), **82%** of urban dwellers worldwide use the internet, against **63%** of the rural population. The gap, measured as the ratio of the two percentages, narrowed slightly from **1.4** in 2023 to **1.3** in 2026; in regions with high internet use such as Europe, the Commonwealth of Independent States and the Americas, the ratio is just **1.1**. In low-income countries, by contrast, only **17%** of rural dwellers are online, against **41%** of urban residents.

Network coverage shows the same picture. Worldwide, **70%** of people living in urban areas have access to a 5G network, against **45%** of those in rural areas: a gap of **25** percentage points. In high-income countries 5G covers **90%** of the urban population and **72%** of the rural population. 4G reaches **99%** of the urban population and **87%** of the rural population. Places with no network at all or only 2G are all rural; the share of the population living in them ranges from **2%** in rural Europe to **21%** in rural Africa.

At farm level the gap is wider still. Mehrabi and colleagues (2021) showed that only **24-37%** of farms smaller than **1** hectare are served by 3G or 4G, against **74-80%** of farms over **200** hectares. Croplands with large yield gaps, climate-stressed locations and food-insecure populations have poor coverage; in many African countries less than about **40%** of farming households have internet access, and the cost of data remains prohibitive.

A systematic review of peer-reviewed studies published between 2020 and 2024 (Miller and colleagues, 2025) lists the critical obstacles to IoT-based sensor networks in agriculture as high infrastructure costs, limited interoperability, connectivity constraints in rural areas, and ethical concerns about transparency and data privacy. According to the authors, policy intervention is needed to ensure fair data ownership, cybersecurity and equitable access to smart farming tools, especially in developing regions.

## Where AI stands

Sensors and the internet of things could be the measurement layer through which AI gathers continuous data from the field; but the connectivity this layer depends on is weakest exactly where it is most needed, in rural areas and on small farms. The obstacles the review lists are also less about algorithms than about cost, interoperability and connectivity.

## Türkiye

In Türkiye, part of rural connectivity is the state's responsibility. According to the Ministry of Transport and Infrastructure, operators have no obligation to serve commercially the rural settlements that are hard to reach because of geography and where infrastructure is costly to build; service is brought to these places under Universal Service Law No. **5369**, with all installation and operating costs covered from public funds. According to the minister's statement of February 2025, service had by then been brought to **3,521** settlements with mobile technology and **2,044** with fixed wireless access, **5,565** settlements in total; settlements with a population between **50** and **1,000** that operators do not cover can be included in these projects. Mobile service for **233** more settlements was planned by the end of 2025; whether that target was met has not been announced.

According to the Information and Communication Technologies Authority (BTK), subscription-based machine-to-machine (M2M) subscriptions numbered about **15.2** million at the end of June 2026. Subscriptions to 5G, launched on 1 April 2026, reached **44.5** million by the end of June; but because some devices or SIM cards are not compatible, those actually able to use the service number **26.1** million. Operators' total fibre length is about **720** thousand kilometres. Subscription figures do not show coverage, and none of them is broken down for agriculture or rural areas.

One official measuring network that runs continuously in the field belongs to meteorology. The General Directorate of Meteorology has **2,059** observation systems; **1,717** of them are automatic weather stations with sensors, data collection and communication units. The directorate turns this network's data into agricultural products such as frost warnings (§17).

In the ministry's 2025 activity report, the 2025 target for the indicator on the number of farms using climate-friendly smart farming technology systems is **410**, and the outcome is **0**. According to the report, the target was not reached because grant schemes under the World Bank-financed Türkiye Climate Smart and Competitive Agricultural Growth Project (TUCSAP) had not yet started; training of trainers was held in **15** pilot provinces. Which technologies the indicator covers has not been specified. The Digital Villages Initiative, launched by TAGEM and FAO in 2023 and covering Albania, Bosnia and Herzegovina, Georgia and Türkiye, was completed in December 2025; in Türkiye a village digital centre was set up in Boyalı, in Ankara's Güdül district.

Academic work is at the level of adoption surveys and prototypes. A survey of **325** farmers in six regions (Guldal, Sanli and Turker, 2025) found that **66** used at least one smart farming technology, **170** did not but wanted to, and **89** did not use any; non-users had smaller landholdings, did not use credit, had limited awareness and owned no tractor. The sample was not drawn at random: it consists of farmers who volunteered at ministry training sessions in the province with the most grants in each region. A study carried out at Çukurova University (Civelek, 2022) developed a tractor tracking system running on a LoRaWAN network: over **6** months, in two trials, the devices generated **49,121** and **52,766** data points, the batteries lasted **95** and **98** days, and position was measured with at most **3** metres of error. The hardware cost **55** dollars at May 2020 prices; a tractor manufacturer's 4G device cost **795** dollars. But the data transmission trials used dummy data; testing on a farm is mentioned in the paper as future work.

What is missing is **a published assessment measuring, in Türkiye, the effect on agriculture of agricultural sensor and IoT networks or of rural connectivity investments.**
