# 21 · Data: ownership, platforms, open data

To the question of who owns the data that come off the field, Europe's answer is not ownership but rights: the consent of whoever generates the data, access to them and the ability to take them elsewhere. Farmers themselves are wary of sharing: in a choice experiment, on average **36%** of farmers were willing to share their farm data with a big data platform. Türkiye has no rule or sector agreement governing farmers' access to their own data and its portability, or data sharing between farmers, machinery makers and digital platforms. The largest register of farm data is held by the state: the area registered in the Farmer Registration System was **16.1** million hectares in 2025.

## What it does

A tractor's position, a harvester's yield map, a sensor's moisture reading, a fertiliser record entered into an app: digital farming produces data at every step. The value of these data emerges not in a single field but when the data of thousands of fields are pooled; AI in agriculture is trained on that accumulation. That is where the question arises: whose data are they — the farmer who generates them, the company that makes the machine, the platform that collects them, or the state that keeps the register? And who may use them, on what terms?

## What the field says

In 2018 Europe's farmer and agri-industry organisations published a joint code of conduct on agricultural data sharing by contractual agreement. The code accepts that data cannot be owned in the same way as physical assets; it grants the farmer the rights over data produced on the farm or during farming operations. Under the code:

- The parties may not use, process or share data without the consent of whoever generated them.
- The data originator has the right to receive data on their operation in a structured, machine-readable format.
- Contracts should use simple and understandable language.
- Data must not be used to assess the originator's ability to pay for a service or machine.

But access to data relevant to the correct functioning of the machinery stays restricted, and compliance with the code is voluntary. Where data are linked, through a contract, a land register or coordinates, to an identifiable person, they count as personal data and fall under Europe's data protection regulation. The EU Data Act cites the code as a precedent and, since 2025, has made a user's access to the data of a connected machine binding.

The ownership framing itself is disputed. According to a legal analysis by Atik (2022), the idea of giving farmers a data ownership right has been adopted through voluntary codes in Europe and the US; but that framing is not adequate to address the sector's real problems, namely data lock-in, data fragmentation, data access problems and farmers' lack of trust, and has the potential to make them worse.

In Turland and Slade's (2020) hypothetical choice experiment, who ran the platform was decisive: farmers were most willing to share their data with university researchers and least willing to share them with government. Yet relatively small financial or non-financial benefits significantly increased participation, even among farmers who stated strong privacy preferences. How data actually flow is shown by the agreements: according to a study of **141** terms of use for agricultural apps (Kaur and Dara, 2023), the agreements are long, complex and hard to understand; **95%** fall into the difficult-to-read category, close to **75%** require university-level education to understand, and some data management practices are not given adequate attention.

On the EU side, the February 2020 data strategy listed agriculture among the strategic fields in which common European data spaces would be created. According to the Commission's page, updated in November 2026, common data spaces are being developed across **14** sectors; in agriculture there are initiatives named AgriDataSpace, Divine, CrackSense, ScaleAgData, AgDataValue, 4Growth, Dig4Live and CEADS. The Commission's aim is for businesses, public administrations and individuals to control the data they generate.

## Where AI stands

AI in agriculture grows on data; and data are shared only when they are trusted. Research shows concrete reasons for mistrust: hard-to-read agreements, restricted access to machine data, who runs the platform. In Europe, a binding regulation has now been added alongside the voluntary code.

## Türkiye

According to the ministry's 2025 activity report, the Farmer Registration System (ÇKS) is the agricultural database in which the areas under crops, the producers and production information are recorded. Registration was made easier through e-Devlet; land actually farmed can be registered with a pledge, regardless of ownership. In **21** provinces, ÇKS applications and data entry were handed by protocol to the Chambers of Agriculture. The area registered in ÇKS reached **93%** of the 2025 target of **17.3** million hectares. The ministry has **185** web-based applications and **46** services on e-Devlet.

According to the 2027 Programme, during the field work of the General Agricultural Census, conducted by the ministry with TÜİK, census offices were set up in **973** localities and about **3.4** million hectares of farmland not registered in ministry systems were entered ex officio into the Agricultural Production Registration System; the census results are expected to be shared with the public by the end of 2026. The Programme foresees a Farm Holding Registration System in which, using the census data, every farm holding will be registered and records will be updated in real time, integrated with spatial data, and the completion of a data centre for agricultural information systems. A legal framework for sharing public data is to be put in place and a national open data portal launched; open government data legislation and the alignment of Law No. **6698** on the Protection of Personal Data with the EU's data protection regulation are also among the tasks for 2027.

[[gorsel:b21-2025-genel-tarim-sayimi-nin-saha]]

The third action of the Türkiye AI Action Plan (2026-2030) aims to provide data access through data spaces, a secure data sharing programme and an open data policy: companies and public institutions will be able to share their data in a controlled way, voluntarily and for mutual benefit, under common rules, standard data-sharing contracts, licensing models and anonymisation standards. Sectoral data roadmaps will be published for health, agriculture, defence and e-commerce; the targets are at least three pilot data spaces, at least **25** shared datasets and at least **2,000** public datasets published in machine-readable form in the National Data Library. Joint agricultural data catalogues with countries of similar climate, language family or sector structure are also foreseen. But the plan does not address data ownership, and it never mentions farmers, rural areas or villages. Nor is there a published study measuring Turkish farmers' willingness to share data or the effect of data platforms on farmers.

There is no legal provision specific to agricultural data. Under Law No. **6698** of 2016, any information relating to an identified or identifiable natural person is personal data and, as a rule, may not be processed without the person's explicit consent; by this definition, parcel or production data that can be linked to a farmer may also count as personal data. The Law does not apply to processing personal data for official statistics, or, once anonymised, for purposes such as research, planning and statistics. The technical requirements of one of the ministry's AI projects include compliance with this Law.
