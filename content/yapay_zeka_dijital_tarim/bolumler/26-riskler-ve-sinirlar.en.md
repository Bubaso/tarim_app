# 26 · Risks and limits

Some of the risks of AI in agriculture have been measured. In Tanzania, the F-1 score of a cassava disease model tested in a field dropped by **67%** on early, mild symptoms. The problem is not only how a model performs in the field: most of the digital automation service providers FAO examined were not profitable and financially sustainable, and those that were mostly served large-scale producers. Electricity use by data centres is growing; in the US, farm cooperatives were hit by ransomware attacks during the harvest season. In Türkiye these risks meet small farms and an ageing farming population.

## When the model leaves the lab

The best-known limit of models that recognise disease from images is that their performance falls outside the conditions they were trained in. Mohanty and colleagues (2016) trained a deep learning model on **54,306** leaf images taken in controlled conditions to recognise **26** diseases in **14** crop species. On test images held out from the same set, the model reached **99.35%** accuracy. But on two small sets (**121** and **119** images) collected from trusted online sources such as agricultural extension services and taken in other conditions, accuracy stayed at **31.40%** and **31.69%**; a random guess among the **38** classes would average **2.63%**. The authors list this as the study's first limitation; the second is that the model can only classify single leaves, facing up, on a plain background. According to the authors, laboratory tests are always more reliable than a diagnosis based on visible symptoms alone, and diagnosing a disease at an early stage by eye alone is often difficult.

Ramcharan and colleagues (2019) put a model that recognises cassava diseases into a phone app and tested it on **720** diseased leaflets in a field in Tanzania. For pronounced symptoms, the F-1 score fell from **0.79** on the test set to **0.54** in field photos (a **32%** drop) and **0.48** in video (a **39%** drop). For early, mild symptoms the F-1 score stood at **0.26** in photos and **0.25** in video. In other words, the model performed worst at the early stage, where diagnosis is most useful. According to the authors, a model running on a phone faces new challenges such as lighting and orientation, and such models must be tested in real-world conditions.

## Who benefits

According to FAO's 2022 State of Food and Agriculture report, the use of digital automation technologies is growing, but mostly in high-income countries. Because data are scarce, FAO carried out **27** case studies based on interviews with companies providing digital automation services: only **10** of them were profitable and financially sustainable. These companies are mostly based in high-income countries, use mature, widely adopted solutions and mostly serve large-scale producers.

FAO names the biggest barriers to adoption as low digital literacy in rural areas, the lack of technologies suited to small-scale producers and the relatively high cost of these technologies; these are often compounded by a reluctance to change, generally associated with ageing farming populations. The report also writes that automation carries the risk of deepening social inequalities: larger and more educated producers have more capacity to invest in new technology, retrain and learn new skills. The adoption data of the US and the EU show the same gap between large and small farms.

## Labour

According to FAO, where rural labour is abundant and wages are low, agricultural automation can lead to unemployment; this can happen especially when subsidies make automation artificially cheap or sudden technological breakthroughs bring its cost down rapidly. But the report writes that common fears that automation leads to growing unemployment, although understandable, are generally not supported by historical realities. The expected change is in the nature of work: routine jobs such as planting and harvesting are expected to decline, and skilled jobs requiring, for example, secondary education to grow.

Focusing on Canada, Rotz and colleagues (2019) use literature and field data to identify three tensions between agricultural production, technology and labour:

- Rising land costs and automation
- A labour market split into high-skilled and low-skilled
- The question of who controls digital data

The authors stress that the current enthusiasm for digital agriculture should not blind us to the specific ways new technologies intensify exploitation and deepen the marginalisation of both labour and places; they draw particular attention to racial exploitation in farm labour.

## Systemic risks and security

Tzachor and colleagues (2022) group the risks of AI in agriculture under three headings:

- The interoperability, reliability and relevance of agricultural data
- The unintended social and ecological consequences of machine learning models optimised for yields alone
- The safety and security concerns of deploying these systems at scale

According to the authors, the effects of machine learning models, expert systems and autonomous machines on farms, farmers and food security are poorly understood. The measures they propose include bringing rural anthropologists and applied ecologists into technology design, setting up data cooperatives for data transparency and ownership rights, and deploying agricultural AI first in digital sandboxes.

The security risk has concrete examples too. According to an April 2022 notice from the US Federal Bureau of Investigation (FBI), ransomware actors may be more likely to attack farm cooperatives during critical seasons such as planting and harvest; because of their time-sensitive role in production, cooperatives may be seen as targets willing to pay. Between 15 September and 6 October 2021, six grain cooperatives were hit by ransomware; some had to halt production completely, others lost administrative functions. Two attacks in early 2022 could have affected the planting season by disrupting the supply of seeds and fertiliser. In July 2021, in an attack on a business management software company, the attacker demanded a ransom of **30** million dollars; the attack spread to several farm cooperatives among the company's clients. The initial entry points the FBI lists include known but unpatched vulnerabilities and infections through shared network resources or externally managed services.

## Energy

According to the International Energy Agency (IEA), data centres used about **1.5%** of the world's electricity in 2024, or **415** terawatt-hours. Their consumption is expected to more than double by 2030, to about **945** terawatt-hours, slightly more than Japan's total electricity consumption today. AI is the most important driver of this growth. Even so, data centres account for about one-tenth of global electricity demand growth to 2030, less than industrial motors, air conditioning in homes and offices, or electric vehicles. In emerging and developing economies, data centres account for about **5%** of the growth in demand to 2030. The IEA's figures cover all data centres; they do not single out agriculture.

## The ground for risk in Türkiye

According to the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry's National Rural Development Strategy (2024-2028), the average farm in the Farmer Registration System was **6.9** hectares in 2023, and has been between **6.9** and **7.1** hectares since 2020. In the same year, **2,245,215** farms and **306,033** young farmers were registered in the system. The strategy writes that Turkish agriculture combines medium and large farms producing for world markets with traditional small family farms producing for local markets and household consumption. Its list of weaknesses includes the ageing of the farming population and young people's declining interest in farming, the small scale of farms, and limited access to smart farming applications. The strategy's measures include increasing the use of AI and smart technology applications and training to raise digital literacy.

A working paper on rural development for the Fourth Agriculture and Forestry Council, based on TÜİK's 2023 labour data, puts the average age of people working in agriculture at **46**, against about **39** across all employment; **68.4%** of agricultural workers are over **40**, compared with **47.1%** in overall employment. Agricultural employment fell from **5** million **220** thousand in 2014 to **4** million **695** thousand in 2023.

According to the 2027 Annual Programme, the small, fragmented and scattered land structure of farms raises production costs and hinders the effective use of mechanisation and modern production techniques. In villages, **1,159,477** people are aged **65** or over. Agriculture's share of total employment was **14%** in 2025 and **13.4%** in the first half of 2026. The programme plans to support the development of the human and technical capacity of farms and producer organisations for smart farming; the results of the 2025 General Agricultural Census are expected to be shared at the end of 2026.

No published official statistic regularly measures to what extent farmers in Türkiye use digital tools and AI. One measurement comes from a 2020 ministry survey: based on it, a 2025 report by FAO and the EBRD states that fewer than **3%** of farmers use smart farming technologies such as remote sensing, sensors, farm or herd management software, precision farming or robotics.
